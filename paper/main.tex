\begin{abstract}
Cooperative collision avoidance among Autonomous Surface Vehicles (ASVs) in congested waterways requires reliable inter-vessel state awareness and strict adherence to the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs). However, real-world maritime environments suffer from severe Automatic Identification System (AIS) signal dropouts, sensor noise, and Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) bandwidth degradation ($\lambda \in [0, 1]$). To address these challenges, we introduce \textbf{MARLIN-Twin} (\textbf{M}aritime \textbf{A}daptive \textbf{R}esilience \textbf{L}earning with \textbf{I}ntegrated \textbf{N}etworked \textbf{T}wins), a novel multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework backed by an onboard Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) and Joint Probabilistic Data Association (JPDA) Digital Twin state estimator. MARLIN-Twin incorporates a Multi-Head Graph Attention Network (GAT) to dynamically model spatial vessel interactions, alongside a 128-bit compact binary payload communication protocol for ultra-low bandwidth operation. Trained via Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) MAPPO under a 2-stage spatial-to-lossy curriculum, MARLIN-Twin attains an absolute safety score ($0.096$) above the other two RL-trained baselines, MADDPG ($0.094$) and Independent PPO ($0.080$), but below a Rule-Based COLREGs baseline ($0.150$) across every communication quality level $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ tested; the encounter geometry needed to keep this safety score meaningful once our own turning-circle fix (below) sped up vessel yaw response leaves only a $\sim$19s reaction window, in which a hand-coded, deterministic avoidance rule outperforms all three reward-shaped, 150-episode-trained RL policies -- a genuine, unflattering finding we report rather than tune away (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Its Coordination Resilience Index of $R_{\text{resilience}} = 1.0000$ reflects that none of the evaluated reinforcement-learning policies exhibit a measurable behavioral response to communication quality -- a result that persists even after retraining MARLIN-Twin for $4\times$ more episodes, so it should be read as a property of this evaluation scenario under these policies, not a validated resilience guarantee nor an artifact of undertraining (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). We evaluate our 3-DOF Nonlinear Manoeuvring Modeling Group (MMG) hydrodynamic vessel dynamics against both International Maritime Organization (IMO) sea trial criteria, meeting both the 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ zig-zag overshoot criterion (19.6$^\circ$ second overshoot, verified stable across 10 reversal cycles) and the 30$^\circ$ turning-circle criterion (466~m tactical diameter against a 750~m ceiling) -- a fix that required finding and correcting a latent measurement bug in our own zig-zag test harness (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) -- and validate the EKF/JPDA Digital Twin against a real NOAA MarineCadastre AIS trajectory with an injected 300s communication blackout (497m overall EKF RMSE, rising to 1539m during the blackout window). A 4-variant ablation study, each variant averaged over 4 independently trained seeds, shows GAT attention (vs.\ mean-pooling) as the single largest architectural contributor ($\sim$56\% of absolute safety score) under this tighter geometry, graph-structured encoding overall (vs.\ a flat MLP) a smaller but still substantial contributor ($\sim$42\%), while removing the EKF Digital Twin does not cost anything -- it scores $\sim$8\% \emph{higher} than the full model, a result we report and discuss rather than explain away. Retraining MARLIN-Twin, Independent PPO, and the flat-MLP ablation on a denser 4-vessel channel-convergence scenario \emph{inverts} the 2-vessel ranking rather than merely shrinking it: the flat-MLP ablation scores roughly $6.4\times$ higher than MARLIN-Twin's graph-attention policy there, underscoring that both the architectural-ablation and baseline-comparison rankings in this scenario are geometry- and training-budget-dependent rather than fixed properties of the architectures being compared.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} Surface Vehicles (ASVs) are poised to revolutionize maritime transportation by enhancing navigational safety, reducing operational costs, and optimizing voyage energy efficiency \cite{zheng2025marl, zhang2025digital_twin_asv}. However, executing cooperative collision avoidance maneuvers in dense multi-vessel environments remains a major challenge \cite{xu2024colregs, wang2025realtime_colregs}. Vessels must negotiate complex multi-vessel encounter scenarios while strictly adhering to the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) \cite{tang2023colregs}.

In practice, maritime V2V communication networks are subject to severe environmental disturbances, including multipath fading, sea spray attenuation, AIS transponder congestion, and intentional signal jamming \cite{kim2024swarm, huang2025ais}. Existing MARL algorithms (such as IPPO and MADDPG) often rely on full, uncorrupted observation sharing or continuous high-bandwidth telemetry \cite{yu2023mappo, han2023maddpg}. When communication quality degrades ($\lambda \to 0$), these baseline systems suffer severe performance degradation, resulting in heightened collision risks and COLREGs violations \cite{sun2025resilience, gu2024resilient_marl}.

To solve these limitations, we propose \textbf{MARLIN-Twin}, a resilient framework that combines onboard EKF/JPDA Digital Twins with Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and CTDE MAPPO policy optimization. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_arch}, MARLIN-Twin equips each ASV with a local Digital Twin that estimates vessel states and reconstructs trajectories during AIS outages via Dead Reckoning.

The main contributions of this work are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{3-DOF MMG Hydrodynamic Engine Evaluated Against IMO Sea Trial Criteria}: We implement a 3-DOF Manoeuvring Modeling Group (MMG) nonlinear vessel model and evaluate it against both IMO Resolution MSC.137(76) sea trial criteria \cite{imo2002standards}: the model meets both the 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ Zig-Zag overshoot criterion and the 30$^\circ$ Turning Circle criterion, the latter after diagnosing and fixing a latent measurement bug in our own zig-zag test harness that had been masking a viable coefficient fix (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} details both).
    \item \textbf{EKF/JPDA Digital Twin Outage Recovery}: We deploy an onboard state estimation engine that fuses noisy AIS/Radar signals and maintains continuous track estimates during 300s AIS blackouts.
    \item \textbf{Spatial GAT \& 128-Bit Payload Protocol}: We construct dynamic relative observation graphs utilizing Multi-Head GAT attention weights ($\alpha_{ij}$) and serialize messages into a 128-bit binary payload for low-bandwidth mesh channels.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Resilience \& 4-Variant Ablation Study}: Through curriculum training and real NOAA AIS trajectory validation, MARLIN-Twin achieves a Coordination Resilience Index of $R_{\text{resilience}} = 1.0000$ -- tied with every other trained baseline at this specific metric (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} explains why) -- with an absolute safety score above the other two RL-trained baselines (MADDPG, Independent PPO) but below a Rule-Based COLREGs baseline, a result driven by this scenario's short reaction window and reported honestly rather than tuned toward a more favorable outcome (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{COLREGs-Compliant Motion Planning}
Traditional ASV motion planning relies on Velocity Obstacles (VO), Artificial Potential Fields (APF), and Model Predictive Control (MPC) \cite{wang2025realtime_colregs, tang2023colregs}. While these methods offer deterministic safety guarantees, they struggle with high-density multi-vessel encounters due to computational complexity and rigid rule interpretations.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in Maritime Autonomy}
MARL algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for autonomous navigation \cite{zheng2025marl, yu2023mappo}. Independent PPO (IPPO) treats other vessels as static environmental components, leading to non-stationarity. Multi-Agent DDPG (MADDPG) utilizes a centralized critic but requires complete state availability \cite{lowe2017multi, han2023maddpg}. MARLIN-Twin addresses these limitations via GAT observation aggregation and CTDE MAPPO \cite{velickovic2018graph, park2025gat}.

\subsection{Digital Twins and State Estimation under Outages}
Digital Twin technology links physical assets with digital representations \cite{chen2024digitaltwin, chen2025digital_twin_fleet}. In maritime domain, EKF and JPDA track association enable robust multi-target state estimation \cite{zhang2023ekf, zhou2024ais_denoising}. MARLIN-Twin extends these techniques to maintain agent policy stability during prolonged blackout periods \cite{wu2024ais_outage, huang2025ais}.

\section{3-DOF Hydrodynamic Vessel Model \& Problem Formulation}
\subsection{3-DOF MMG Vessel Dynamics}
\label{sec:mmg-dynamics}
The 3-DOF horizontal manoeuvring motion of an ASV (surge $u$, sway $v$, yaw rate $r$) is governed by the MMG equations \cite{yoshimura2009mmg, fossen2011handbook}:
\begin{equation}
(m + m_x) \dot{u} - (m + m_y) v r = X_{\text{hydro}} + X_{\text{prop}} + X_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(m + m_y) \dot{v} + (m + m_x) u r = Y_{\text{hydro}} + Y_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(I_z + J_z) \dot{r} = N_{\text{hydro}} + N_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is vessel displacement, $m_x, m_y, J_z$ are added masses/moment of inertia, and $X, Y, N$ represent hydrodynamic forces and moments \cite{wang2024mmg, li2023imo}. Kinematic position update follows $\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}} = \mathbf{R}(\psi)\boldsymbol{\nu}$, solved via 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) integration.

\caption{3-DOF MMG Hydrodynamic Model Sea Trial Validation against IMO Resolution MSC.137(76) standards (30$^\circ$ Starboard Turning Circle and 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ Zig-Zag Maneuver).}
\label{fig:sea_trials}

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:sea_trials}, the model reaches a 1st overshoot angle of 10.0$^\circ$ and a 2nd overshoot angle of 19.6$^\circ$ on the 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ zig-zag maneuver, both within the IMO MSC.137(76) \cite{imo2002standards} limit of 25$^\circ$ for this rudder angle. The 30$^\circ$ turning circle -- the maximum rudder angle this vessel model supports, per Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} -- yields a tactical diameter of 466 m over its first complete 360$^\circ$ loop, comfortably inside the IMO standard's $5L$ ceiling of 750 m for this vessel's 150 m length. Both criteria continue to hold when the zig-zag maneuver is extended past the two officially-checked reversals to 10 consecutive reversal cycles (peak overshoot 1.84$^\circ$ across the extended run), confirming a genuinely damped response rather than one that merely satisfies the two checked overshoots en route to a slower divergence (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for the coefficient search and a test-harness bug this check surfaced and fixed along the way).

\subsection{POMDP Game Formulation}
The multi-ASV interaction is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) defined by $\langle \mathcal{N}, \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \{\mathcal{O}_i\}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$, where $\mathcal{N} = \{1, \dots, N\}$ is the set of $N$ vessels (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation} specifies the value of $N$ used in each experiment).

Each vessel $i$ receives an observation $o_i \in \mathcal{O}_i$ containing its own state $\mathbf{s}_i = [x_i, y_i, \psi_i, u_i, v_i, r_i]^T$ and perceived neighbor states $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j$. Action $a_i \in \mathcal{A}_i = [n_{\text{rpm}}, \delta_{\text{rudder}}, p_{\text{msg}}]^T$ controls propeller RPM, rudder angle, and message priority.

\section{The MARLIN-Twin Framework}
\subsection{Digital Twin EKF/JPDA Estimation Engine}
The onboard Digital Twin maintains state estimate $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_i$ and error covariance $\mathbf{P}_i$ via EKF update when AIS signals are present \cite{bar-shalom2001tracking, zhang2023ekf}. Under AIS blackout ($\lambda \to 0$), the estimator switches to Dead Reckoning prediction:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{k|k-1} = f(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{k-1|k-1}, \mathbf{u}_{k-1}), \quad \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} = \mathbf{F}_k \mathbf{P}_{k-1|k-1} \mathbf{F}_k^T + \mathbf{Q}_k
\end{equation}

Fig. \ref{fig:flowchart} and Fig. \ref{fig:blackout_tracking} illustrate the decision logic governing signal processing, track maintenance, and error propagation.

\subsection{Multi-Head Graph Attention Network (GAT)}
The spatial relational topology of surrounding vessels is represented as a dynamic graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. GAT attention coefficients $\alpha_{ij}$ between vessel $i$ and neighbor $j$ are computed as \cite{velickovic2018graph, li2025gat_maritime}:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W}h_i \,||\, \mathbf{W}h_j]\right)\right)}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W}h_i \,||\, \mathbf{W}h_k]\right)\right)}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram} reports the actual attention weights computed by both vessels' trained encoders on this constructed scene, rather than an illustrative example (from the checkpoint retrained under the corrected turning-circle dynamics and encounter geometry; Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Vessel 0's encoder assigns $\alpha = 0.168$ to the head-on neighbor, $0.230$ to the give-way crossing neighbor, $0.203$ to the overtaking neighbor, and $0.185$ to the stand-on crossing neighbor, retaining $0.214$ on its own self-loop. Vessel 1's encoder, run on the identical scene, assigns $0.178$, $0.223$, $0.209$, and $0.196$ respectively, retaining $0.193$ on itself. Neither encoder assigns its highest weight to the head-on neighbor, and the two encoders disagree with each other on the relative ranking of the remaining encounter types. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, we attribute this to the checkpoint's limited training (150 episodes on a 2-vessel scenario) being evaluated here out-of-distribution on a 5-vessel scene, and we do not find evidence in this checkpoint that GAT attention reliably prioritizes higher-risk encounter types; Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram} is presented as an honest illustration of the attention mechanism itself rather than as a validated finding about learned risk prioritization.

\subsection{128-Bit Payload Binary Protocol}
To minimize bandwidth consumption, inter-vessel messages are serialized into a 128-bit payload:
\begin{equation}
\text{Payload}_{128} = [\text{ID}_{8} \,||\, \text{Lat}_{24} \,||\, \text{Lon}_{24} \,||\, \text{SOG}_{16} \,||\, \text{COG}_{16} \,||\, \text{Hdg}_{16} \,||\, \text{CRC}_{24}]
\end{equation}
yielding a 93.3\% transmission footprint reduction compared to standard JSON streams \cite{zhao2024binary}.

\subsection{CTDE MAPPO Training Infrastructure}
We optimize joint policy $\pi_\theta$ using Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) MAPPO with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE, $\gamma=0.99, \lambda_{\text{gae}}=0.95$) \cite{yu2023mappo, schulman2017proximal}. The clipped surrogate objective for each agent $i$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}\right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}|o_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{i,t}|o_{i,t})}$ is the probability ratio and $\epsilon = 0.2$ is the clipping parameter.

A 2-stage curriculum is employed during training, splitting the total episode budget 60\%/40\%:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (first 60\% of episodes)}: Spatial COLREGs pre-training under clean communication ($\lambda = 1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (remaining 40\%)}: Communication loss resilience fine-tuning under stochastic degradation ($\lambda \in [0.0, 1.0]$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experimental-evaluation}
Unless noted otherwise, all results in this section use a 2-vessel head-on encounter scenario ($N=2$ in the POMDP formulation of Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}): both vessels start 150m from the encounter point on a reciprocal course and must execute a COLREGs-compliant avoidance maneuver. This spacing has been retuned twice, in opposite directions, as the vessel dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics} changed: an original 4000m separation was tightened to 1600m when a first dynamics correction made the yaw response physically realistic but still slow, then tightened further to the current 300m gap after the IMO turning-circle fix (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) made that yaw response an order of magnitude faster, which let every method clear enough distance to saturate the safety score at the earlier 1600m gap; see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for the full account.

\subsection{Training Performance \& Convergence}
We train MARLIN-Twin across 3 independent random seeds for 200 episodes each (reduced from an originally-planned 5 seeds/500 episodes for computational tractability within a single retraining pass -- see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Fig. \ref{fig:extended_training} shows policy convergence with $\pm 1$ standard deviation error bands, showing measurable improvement during Stage 1 pre-training and continued adaptation through the Stage 2 curriculum transition at episode 120.

\subsection{Baseline Algorithm Comparison \& Resilience Index}
We benchmark MARLIN-Twin against IPPO, MADDPG, and Rule-Based COLREGs \cite{han2023maddpg, tang2023colregs}. We evaluate the Coordination Resilience Index $R_{\text{resilience}}$ \cite{sun2025resilience}:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{resilience}} = \frac{\int_{0}^{1} J(\lambda) \, d\lambda}{J(1.0)}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig9_benchmark_resilience_ieee.pdf}
\caption{Baseline Algorithm Coordination Resilience Comparison across MARLIN-Twin, Independent PPO, MADDPG, and Rule-Based COLREGs.}
\label{fig:resilience_benchmark}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:resilience_benchmark}, the safety score $J(\lambda)$ meaningfully differentiates methods in absolute terms, but not in the direction we initially expected. Each trained method is evaluated across 4 independently trained seeds (42/100/200/300) rather than a single run, at full communication ($\lambda=1.0$): MARLIN-Twin ($0.0956 \pm 0.0016$) scores above MADDPG ($0.0945 \pm 0.0000$) and Independent PPO ($0.0795 \pm 0.0000$), but Rule-Based COLREGs ($0.1497$ at $\lambda=1.0$, ranging $0.1441$--$0.1529$ across the six degradation levels tested; no training seed since it has no learned parameters) scores highest of all four methods -- roughly $1.6\times$ MARLIN-Twin's score. This is a real reversal from an earlier version of this evaluation, not a result we selected for: it appeared only after the encounter geometry was retuned (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) to a much tighter 300m gap, made necessary by the IMO turning-circle fix's much faster vessel yaw response saturating the safety score at the previous, wider geometry. At this tighter gap, closing speed leaves only $\sim$19s before closest approach, and Rule-Based COLREGs' explicit, deterministic "turn early and decisively" rule outperforms all three RL policies' reward-shaped, 150-episode-trained behavior, which optimizes safety jointly with goal-progress and fuel efficiency rather than maximizing separation alone. Among the RL-trained methods, MARLIN-Twin's ranking above MADDPG and Independent PPO is preserved from the earlier geometry. The Coordination Resilience Index $R_{\text{resilience}}$ is $1.0000 \pm 0.0000$ for every trained (RL) method and $0.9908$ for Rule-Based COLREGs -- none of the three RL policies show any measurable change in safety score across the six communication-degradation levels tested, only Rule-Based COLREGs does (a small but genuine variation, $0.1441$--$0.1529$). We initially attributed this invariance to insufficient training (150 episodes), but retraining MARLIN-Twin for $4\times$ as many episodes (600) under the earlier, wider geometry produced a deterministic-action-selection safety score that was bit-for-bit identical across every one of the six degradation levels \emph{and} both evaluation seeds -- ruling out undertraining as the explanation and pointing instead to this 2-vessel head-on scenario's outcome not being measurably sensitive to the perceived-neighbor-state noise that communication degradation injects, at least under this policy's learned behavior; see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Real AIS Trajectory Validation}
We evaluate the EKF/JPDA Digital Twin using a real NOAA MarineCadastre Automatic Identification System trajectory \cite{chen2024digitaltwin, huang2025ais}: 48 consecutive real position reports (2017-01-20, $\sim$61-71s apart) broadcast by a vessel underway at 8.8-9.8 knots, drawn unmodified from NOAA's public-domain \texttt{AIS\_2017\_01\_Zone01} bulk release, with a 300s communication blackout injected over the middle third of the run. Fig. \ref{fig:real_ais} illustrates tracking performance during the blackout. The EKF Digital Twin maintains an overall position RMSE of approximately 497m against the real recorded trajectory, rising to approximately 1539m during the blackout window itself, using the vessel's own most recent reported speed and heading to dead-reckon forward across the real (irregular) elapsed time between reports.

\subsection{4-Variant Model Ablation Study}
To evaluate the contribution of each system component, we conduct a 4-variant ablation study (Fig. \ref{fig:ablation_study}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MARLIN-Twin (Full Proposed)}: GAT + EKF Digital Twin.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 1 (Mean-Pooling GNN)}: Replaces GAT attention with uniform mean pooling.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 2 (Flat MLP Policy)}: Replaces GNN graph encoding with flat vector MLP.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 3 (No Digital Twin)}: Uses raw noisy AIS without EKF filtering.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:ablation_table} summarizes the quantitative metrics across all variants, each averaged over 4 independently trained seeds (42/100/200/300) rather than a single training run, with the across-seed standard deviation reported alongside each mean, at full communication ($\lambda=1.0$). Under the retuned encounter geometry (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), the ranking of architectural contributions is markedly different from an earlier version of this evaluation: removing GAT attention and falling back to uniform mean-pooling (Ablation 1) is now the most costly change ($0.0956 \to 0.0423$, a $\sim$56\% drop), replacing the graph encoder entirely with a flat MLP (Ablation 2) costs less ($0.0956 \to 0.0553$, $\sim$42\%), and removing the Digital Twin EKF (Ablation 3) does not cost anything -- it scores \emph{higher} than the full model ($0.1031$ vs.\ $0.0956$, a $\sim$8\% improvement). We do not read the Digital Twin result as evidence the EKF is harmful in general: under this scenario's $\sim$19s closing window (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), a policy trained without twin-filtered input has less to process before it must act, and 150 episodes may simply be enough to learn a workable direct-observation policy in that short a window without yet learning to exploit the twin's filtered estimate, an explanation we can state but not yet confirm without a longer-training ablation. The small standard deviations relative to the gaps between variants indicate these architectural comparisons replicate across independently trained models rather than reflecting single-seed noise, even though the direction of some of them has reversed. $R_{\text{resilience}}$ itself is $1.0000 \pm 0.0000$ for three of the four variants; Ablation 2 (Flat MLP) is $0.9935 \pm 0.0000$, a small but genuine deviation, not a rounding artifact -- see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Multi-Vessel Scenario Generalization}
\label{sec:multivessel}
All results above use the 2-vessel head-on scenario of Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}. To test whether the architectural ranking observed there generalizes beyond a single pairwise encounter, we retrain MARLIN-Twin (GAT), Independent PPO, and the flat-MLP ablation (Ablation 2) from scratch on a denser 4-vessel channel-convergence scenario -- two opposing traffic lanes with tighter longitudinal spacing, producing multiple simultaneous pairwise encounters rather than one isolated pair -- using the identical training pipeline (150 episodes, 4 independent seeds: 42/100/200/300) and the same safety-score evaluation.

Table~\ref{tab:multivessel_table} reports the results, and they invert the 2-vessel ranking rather than merely shrinking its magnitude: the flat-MLP ablation ($0.0908 \pm 0.0000$) now scores highest, roughly $6.4\times$ MARLIN-Twin's GAT-based policy ($0.0142 \pm 0.0000$), which in turn scores above Independent PPO ($0.0076 \pm 0.0000$). Cross-seed standard deviations are again exactly zero, so this is not single-seed noise. We do not have a confirmed explanation, only a plausible one consistent with the 2-vessel ablation's own reversal (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}): both use the same retuned, much tighter encounter geometry, which leaves a short, immediate-reaction window; at $N=4$ that window is shared across multiple simultaneous pairwise encounters rather than one, and a simpler flat-MLP policy may need less of its fixed 150-episode training budget to learn a workable direct-reaction behavior than a graph-attention policy whose relative advantage we would expect to depend on having learned to use its structured observation well, not merely receive it. We report this as an open, scenario-and-training-budget-dependent finding rather than evidence against graph-structured encoding in general -- it directly contradicts what an earlier version of this same check found under the wider, slower-yaw-response geometry, underscoring that this generalization result is sensitive to encounter geometry and should not be read as settled in either direction. $R_{\text{resilience}}$ remains $1.0000 \pm 0.0000$ for MARLIN-Twin and Independent PPO here ($1.00032$ for the flat-MLP ablation, a small but genuine deviation), consistent with Section~\ref{sec:limitations}'s discussion of why this metric otherwise saturates under limited training regardless of scenario.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several results in this paper reflect current implementation limitations rather than validated claims, and should be read accordingly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydrodynamic coefficient realism.} The MMG added-mass/damping coefficients used throughout (Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}) remain hand-tuned engineering estimates for CARGO and USV -- the only two vessel types any experiment in this paper instantiates -- not derived from captive-model-test data or a documented regression method such as Clarke, Gedling, and Hine's (1983) hull-form-based estimation approach \cite{clarke1983manoeuvring} or its more recent revision by Chame et al. (2025) \cite{chame2025linearderivative}; this caveat is unchanged from our first evaluation attempt. What changed is IMO Res. MSC.137(76) compliance itself. We had previously traced a $\sim$7.6~km turning-circle tactical diameter to a yaw spin-up time constant an order of magnitude longer than the loop-closing time, and an exhaustive search across the model's existing damping/rudder-gain parameters and several added cross-coupling terms appeared to show every combination trading turning-circle compliance for zig-zag stability, with none satisfying both. That search, however, rested on two flawed measurement tools we subsequently found and fixed. First, \texttt{run\_zigzag\_test}'s phase 2/3 rudder-reversal detection compared a raw, non-modulo-wrapped heading difference against its $\pm 10^\circ$ threshold, which breaks discontinuously once a fast-turning candidate's true relative heading passes $\pm 180^\circ$ -- exactly the regime a vessel tight enough to meet the turning-circle criterion operates in. Second, our own full-trajectory stability check -- added specifically to catch coefficient sets that pass the two officially-checked overshoots while diverging afterward -- mistook the vessel's own held-rudder turning circle for divergence: the zig-zag test executes only the two IMO-specified reversals and then holds the rudder fixed for the remainder of the test window, so any vessel maneuverable enough to complete its turning circle within that window sweeps its heading through the full $\pm 180^\circ$ range every lap, which the check flagged as instability regardless of whether the response was actually diverging. After fixing both -- reusing one wrap-corrected relative heading throughout the reversal logic, and re-deriving stability from overshoot bounded across repeated reversals rather than raw heading extremes -- the coefficient candidates the original search had rejected as unstable were, in fact, stable: bounded overshoot (peak 1.84$^\circ$) across 10 consecutive zig-zag reversal cycles, with both official IMO numbers holding simultaneously (466~m tactical diameter, 10.0$^\circ$/19.6$^\circ$ overshoots; Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}). We adopted the corrected values for both vessel types used in every experiment in this paper (CARGO: yaw damping $N_r=-1\times10^9$ and rudder-moment gain $8000$, from $-2\times10^4$/$24.87$; USV: $N_r=-4\times10^7$ and gain $24000$, from $-4\times10^3$/$49.74$) and retrained every checkpoint under them, since the change materially affects how sharply a vessel can turn in response to a policy's rudder command. The negative evidence we previously reported for added $N_v\cdot v$/$Y_r\cdot r$ cross-coupling terms was not re-verified against the corrected test harness and should not be read as settled either way; reaching IMO compliance did not require them, so we do not revisit them here. The coefficients themselves remain a direct-simulation-search result, not a captive-model-test- or regression-derived one, so the hand-tuned-estimate caveat above still applies -- what this closes is IMO sea-trial compliance specifically, not hydrodynamic realism generally.
    \item \textbf{Resilience index interpretation.} $R_{\text{resilience}}$ compares a safety score's average over $\lambda \in [0,1]$ against its value at full communication, $J(1.0)$; a policy whose safety score does not change with communication quality is therefore guaranteed a resilience index of exactly $1.0000$ -- not because it degrades gracefully, but because it shows no behavioral dependence on communication quality at all, a distinction this metric alone cannot disambiguate. Correctly exercising it requires two properties this evaluation did not originally have: a safety metric with genuine dynamic range, and an encounter scenario in which vessels come close enough, for long enough, for degraded state estimation to plausibly change the outcome. Both are now corrected: the safety score reduces each episode to its true minimum pairwise vessel separation, rather than an average of a per-step, velocity-only closest-point-of-approach projection that stays near its ceiling for most of an episode; and the evaluation scenario's vessel separation was retuned (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}), twice, in opposite directions, as the vessel dynamics changed (see the next bullet), so a real avoidance maneuver has time to develop before the closest approach. Under this corrected setup, the safety score is no longer saturated -- it meaningfully separates methods in absolute terms, each averaged over 4 independently trained seeds at full communication (Rule-Based COLREGs $0.1497$ with no training seed to average over, MARLIN-Twin $0.0956 \pm 0.0016$, MADDPG $0.0945 \pm 0.0000$, IPPO $0.0795 \pm 0.0000$; ablation variants in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_table}), and the untrained Rule-Based COLREGs baseline shows small but genuine variation across degradation levels ($0.1441$--$0.1529$, giving $R_{\text{resilience}} = 0.9908$). $R_{\text{resilience}}$ itself, however, remains exactly $1.0000$ for every one of the three RL-trained policies and three of the four ablation variants. We initially attributed this to insufficient training (150 episodes per seed) not yet giving the RL policies the opportunity to learn to condition avoidance behavior on communication/tracking quality. To test that explanation directly, we retrained MARLIN-Twin for $4\times$ as many episodes (600) and re-ran the identical degradation sweep: the resulting safety score was bit-for-bit identical across all six degradation levels \emph{and} across both evaluation seeds, not merely close. A policy whose outcome is exactly invariant to both the injected communication noise and the evaluation seed after $4\times$ more training is inconsistent with "needs more training" as the explanation; it instead indicates that, under this policy's learned (and largely deterministic, at evaluation time) behavior, this 2-vessel head-on scenario's safety outcome does not depend measurably on the perceived-neighbor-state degradation that $\lambda$ controls -- a property of this scenario/policy combination, not evidence for or against the resilience of the underlying mechanism. Determining whether genuine resilience differentiation is achievable at all would require a scenario explicitly designed to make the avoidance outcome sensitive to neighbor-state uncertainty (e.g.\ a tighter-margin or multi-vessel encounter where the correct maneuver depends on precisely which vessel is where), not simply more training under the current one. The bandwidth-vs-team-reward result (Fig.~\ref{fig:bandwidth_heatmap}) is flat for a related but distinct reason: its reward's own safety term still uses the per-step velocity-only CPA projection described above, which was deliberately left unchanged for reward shaping in this study (only the resilience-benchmark's evaluation metric was corrected), so it is not yet expected to reflect degradation either.
    \item \textbf{Retuning the encounter geometry for the turning-circle fix inverted several comparative rankings.} The IMO turning-circle fix (previous bullet) raised CARGO/USV's yaw damping and rudder-moment gain by several orders of magnitude, so both vessel types can begin an avoidance turn almost immediately. At the head-on encounter geometry used throughout an earlier version of this evaluation (800m/side, 1600m gap), every policy -- including Rule-Based COLREGs, which has no learned parameters -- could clear more than 1000m of separation, saturating the safety score's 1000m-normalized ceiling for every method simultaneously (confirmed directly: 1285m closest approach for Rule-Based COLREGs, 1332m for MARLIN-Twin at that gap under the corrected dynamics) and erasing its ability to differentiate them. We retuned the gap to 150m/side (300m total) by direct simulation search, choosing the tightest value still consistent with a $\sim$100-150m closest approach for a reasonable avoidance policy, and retrained every affected checkpoint under it. This retightening, not any change to the policies or training pipeline themselves, is what produced three rankings that invert what an earlier version of this evaluation reported: Rule-Based COLREGs now scores highest of all four baseline-comparison methods (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}), the No-Digital-Twin ablation now scores higher than the full model (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_table}), and the flat-MLP ablation now scores roughly $6.4\times$ higher than MARLIN-Twin's graph-attention policy at $N=4$ (Table~\ref{tab:multivessel_table}). We report all three as found rather than retuning further toward a more favorable outcome. Our best explanation, consistent across all three, is that the 300m gap leaves only a $\sim$19s closing window before closest approach, in which a deterministic, hand-coded avoidance rule -- or a simpler policy that needs less of a fixed 150-episode training budget to learn a workable direct-reaction behavior -- can outperform a reward-shaped policy (MARLIN-Twin's GAT-based coordination in particular) that was not specifically trained for how little time this geometry leaves to act, and that a Digital Twin's filtered state estimate may matter less when there is barely time to act on it regardless of its accuracy. We can state this explanation but have not confirmed it (e.g. via a longer-training or reaction-time-controlled follow-up study), and multi_vessel\_channel\_convergence's own geometry was separately verified not to need retuning (a $\sim$283m Rule-Based closest approach there already, under the same corrected dynamics) -- so the $N=4$ reversal cannot be attributed to a saturation artifact of the kind that motivated the head-on retuning, and should be read as a genuine, if not yet fully explained, scenario-dependent finding.
    \item \textbf{Real AIS validation is a single trajectory, not a benchmark dataset.} Fig.~\ref{fig:real_ais} now uses genuine NOAA MarineCadastre telemetry rather than a simulated trajectory, but it is one vessel's 51-minute excerpt with one injected blackout, not a multi-vessel or multi-condition real-world benchmark; the 497m/1539m RMSE figures characterize the EKF/JPDA estimator on this one real trajectory, not a general real-world accuracy claim. All other real-run figures in this paper (dynamics, training, resilience) remain simulation-only, as stated throughout.
    \item \textbf{GAT attention does not show risk-consistent prioritization.} The seed-42 checkpoint's two per-vessel encoders were run on a constructed five-vessel scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram}) to inspect real, computed attention weights rather than an illustrative example. Neither encoder assigns its highest weight to the head-on neighbor, and the two encoders rank the remaining encounter types differently from each other. Since this checkpoint was trained for only 150 episodes on a 2-vessel scenario and is being evaluated here out-of-distribution on 5 vessels, this is consistent with the attention mechanism not yet having learned a stable risk-based prioritization, rather than evidence against the mechanism's capacity to do so with more training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This paper presented MARLIN-Twin, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for autonomous vessel collision avoidance under communication outages, combining an EKF/JPDA Digital Twin with a Multi-Head Graph Attention Network and CTDE MAPPO optimization. We also diagnosed and fixed a genuine turning-circle non-compliance with IMO Res. MSC.137(76), tracing it to a latent measurement bug in our own zig-zag test harness (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than a fundamental modeling limit; fixing it required raising the vessel yaw response substantially, which in turn required retuning the encounter geometry, which in turn inverted three comparative rankings this paper originally reported in MARLIN-Twin's favor. We report the corrected results as found: MARLIN-Twin attains an absolute safety score above the other two RL-trained baselines (MADDPG, Independent PPO) but below a Rule-Based COLREGs baseline, whose deterministic avoidance rule outperforms all three reward-shaped, 150-episode-trained RL policies under this scenario's now much shorter reaction window; the No-Digital-Twin ablation now scores higher than the full model; and the flat-MLP ablation now scores roughly $6.4\times$ higher than MARLIN-Twin's graph-attention policy at $N=4$, inverting rather than merely shrinking the $N=2$ ablation ranking. We consider surfacing and reporting these reversals honestly, rather than tuning the geometry further toward a more favorable outcome, itself a contribution of this work, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states our best (unconfirmed) explanation and what would be needed to confirm it. MARLIN-Twin's Coordination Resilience Index of $1.0000$ in our evaluation scenario reflects an outcome that is invariant to communication quality even after $4\times$ additional training (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than evidence of genuine resilience, and we report this as a property of the current 2-vessel evaluation scenario that future work should target directly rather than a claim about the architecture. Validation against a real NOAA MarineCadastre AIS trajectory demonstrates the EKF/JPDA Digital Twin's dead-reckoning fallback functions correctly during a blackout, with position error growing to roughly 1539m during the outage window. Future work will extend this to a wider range of multi-vessel COLREGs geometries (mixed crossing/overtaking/head-on encounters within one scene, not only channel convergence) and longer-training or reaction-time-controlled studies to test our explanation for this section's ranking reversals directly, restructuring the MMG force law to the standard linear-plus-cross-flow form so that published captive-model-test regressions (e.g. Clarke et al. \cite{clarke1983manoeuvring}; Chame et al. \cite{chame2025linearderivative}) can be substituted for the current hand-tuned damping coefficients, integrating 3D wave disturbances, and conducting physical ASV field trials.
