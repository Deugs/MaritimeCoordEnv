\begin{abstract}
Cooperative collision avoidance among Autonomous Surface Vehicles (ASVs) requires reliable inter-vessel state awareness and strict COLREGs adherence despite Automatic Identification System (AIS) dropouts and Vehicle-to-Vehicle bandwidth degradation ($\lambda \in [0,1]$). We present \textbf{MARLIN-Twin}, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework combining an onboard EKF/JPDA Digital Twin, Multi-Head Graph Attention (GAT) observation encoding, and CTDE MAPPO, together with a validated 3-DOF MMG hydrodynamic simulation testbed, and use both to evaluate -- rather than assume -- how well this design delivers degradation-resilient collision avoidance. We diagnose and fix a genuine IMO Res. MSC.137(76) turning-circle non-compliance, tracing it to a latent measurement bug in our own test harness rather than a physical limit; the fix (466m tactical diameter against a 750m ceiling; 10.0$^\circ$/19.6$^\circ$ zig-zag overshoots against L/V-dependent limits of 14.4$^\circ$/31.6$^\circ$) required an order-of-magnitude faster vessel yaw response, which in turn required retightening the evaluation encounter's geometry, which in turn exposed a second, more consequential bug: a missing per-seed re-initialization that had made 5 of 6 baseline/ablation policy variants' "independently trained" checkpoints bit-for-bit identical across seeds. After fixing both and retraining under genuine per-seed randomization, most fine-grained architectural rankings this evaluation initially reported do not survive real cross-seed variance and are reported as inconclusive rather than settled. Two findings do hold: Rule-Based COLREGs achieves a safety score (0.150) reproducibly above all three RL-trained methods (0.080-0.091, overlapping $\pm1\sigma$ ranges) in this tight, $\sim$19s-reaction-window encounter; and replacing GAT attention with uniform mean-pooling scores consistently higher (0.116) than the full attention-based model (0.080). A Digital-Twin ablation produces literally identical trained weights to the full model, because this scenario never exercises conditions under which the twin's filtering would change behavior -- consistent with every trained policy's Coordination Resilience Index being invariant to communication quality ($R_{\text{resilience}}=1.0000$). We validate the EKF/JPDA Digital Twin against a real NOAA MarineCadastre AIS trajectory (497m overall RMSE, 1539m during an injected 300s blackout). We report our own seeding and measurement-harness bugs, and the statistical conclusions that changed once we found and fixed them, as a central part of this paper's contribution: an evaluation's comparative claims are only as trustworthy as its randomization and measurement infrastructure, and we would rather demonstrate that discipline than report a cleaner-looking but unverified result.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} Surface Vehicles (ASVs) are poised to revolutionize maritime transportation by enhancing navigational safety and enabling cooperative maneuvering \cite{zheng2025marl}. However, executing cooperative collision avoidance maneuvers in dense multi-vessel environments remains a major challenge \cite{xu2024colregs, wang2025realtime_colregs}. Vessels must negotiate complex multi-vessel encounter scenarios while strictly adhering to the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) \cite{tang2023colregs}.

In practice, maritime V2V communication networks are subject to severe environmental disturbances, including multipath fading, sea spray attenuation, AIS transponder congestion, and intentional signal jamming \cite{alqurashi2024maritime}. Existing MARL algorithms (such as IPPO and MADDPG) often rely on full, uncorrupted observation sharing or continuous high-bandwidth telemetry \cite{hu2023eventtriggered}. When communication quality degrades ($\lambda \to 0$), these baseline systems suffer severe performance degradation, resulting in heightened collision risks and COLREGs violations \cite{gu2024resilient_marl}.

Toward these limitations, we present \textbf{MARLIN-Twin}, a multi-agent framework and validated simulation testbed that combines onboard EKF/JPDA Digital Twins with Graph Attention Networks (GAT) and CTDE MAPPO policy optimization, and use it to evaluate how well this architecture -- and the graph-attention/digital-twin design choices within it -- actually deliver degradation-resilient collision avoidance, rather than assume the answer. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_arch}, MARLIN-Twin equips each ASV with a local Digital Twin that estimates vessel states and reconstructs trajectories during AIS outages via Dead Reckoning; Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation} reports where this design measurably helps and where, under the encounter geometry and training budget tested here, it does not.

The main contributions of this work are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{3-DOF MMG Hydrodynamic Engine Evaluated Against IMO Sea Trial Criteria}: We implement a 3-DOF Manoeuvring Modeling Group (MMG) nonlinear vessel model and evaluate it against both IMO Resolution MSC.137(76) sea trial criteria \cite{imo2002standards}: the model meets both the 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ Zig-Zag overshoot criterion and the 30$^\circ$ Turning Circle criterion, the latter after diagnosing and fixing a latent measurement bug in our own zig-zag test harness that had been masking a viable coefficient fix (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} details both).
    \item \textbf{EKF/JPDA Digital Twin Outage Recovery}: We deploy an onboard state estimation engine that fuses noisy AIS/Radar signals and maintains continuous track estimates during 300s AIS blackouts.
    \item \textbf{Spatial GAT \& 128-Bit Payload Protocol}: We construct dynamic relative observation graphs utilizing Multi-Head GAT attention weights ($\alpha_{ij}$) and serialize messages into a 128-bit binary payload for low-bandwidth mesh channels.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Resilience \& 4-Variant Ablation Study, Including Two Infrastructure Bugs We Found and Fixed}: Through curriculum training and real NOAA AIS trajectory validation, MARLIN-Twin achieves a Coordination Resilience Index of $R_{\text{resilience}} = 1.0000$ -- tied with every other trained baseline at this specific metric (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} explains why). Its absolute safety score is statistically indistinguishable from the other two RL-trained baselines (MADDPG, Independent PPO) but reproducibly below a Rule-Based COLREGs baseline; a related ablation finding shows mean-pooling reproducibly outperforming GAT attention in this scenario. We report both as found. Reaching this point required finding and fixing a missing per-seed re-initialization bug that had silently made most of this evaluation's "independently trained" checkpoints identical across seeds, which we document alongside the results themselves (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than only the results that survived it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{COLREGs-Compliant Motion Planning}
Traditional ASV motion planning relies on Velocity Obstacles (VO), Artificial Potential Fields (APF), and Model Predictive Control (MPC) \cite{wang2025realtime_colregs, xu2024colregs}. These methods offer deterministic safety guarantees and, as this paper's own results show (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}), can be highly effective in a single, well-specified pairwise encounter; the case against them is scalability and flexibility as encounter density and rule ambiguity grow (computational complexity and rigid rule interpretation under high-density multi-vessel encounters), not that they are unconditionally worse than learned approaches.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in Maritime Autonomy}
MARL algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for autonomous navigation \cite{zheng2025marl, yu2023mappo}. Independent PPO (IPPO) treats other vessels as static environmental components, leading to non-stationarity. Multi-Agent DDPG (MADDPG) utilizes a centralized critic but requires complete state availability \cite{lowe2017multi}. MARLIN-Twin investigates whether GAT observation aggregation and CTDE MAPPO \cite{velickovic2018graph, park2025gat} can address these limitations; Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation} reports the extent to which they do.

\subsection{Digital Twins and State Estimation under Outages}
Digital Twin technology links physical assets with digital representations \cite{chen2024digitaltwin, chen2025digital_twin_fleet, zhang2025digital_twin_asv}. In maritime domain, EKF and JPDA track association enable robust multi-target state estimation \cite{zhang2023ekf}. MARLIN-Twin extends these techniques to maintain agent policy stability during prolonged blackout periods \cite{wu2024ais_outage, huang2025ais, zhou2024ais_denoising}.

\section{3-DOF Hydrodynamic Vessel Model \& Problem Formulation}
\subsection{3-DOF MMG Vessel Dynamics}
\label{sec:mmg-dynamics}
The 3-DOF horizontal manoeuvring motion of an ASV (surge $u$, sway $v$, yaw rate $r$) is governed by the MMG equations \cite{yoshimura2009mmg, fossen2011handbook, yasukawa2015mmg}:
\begin{equation}
(m + m_x) \dot{u} - (m + m_y) v r = X_{\text{hydro}} + X_{\text{prop}} + X_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(m + m_y) \dot{v} + (m + m_x) u r = Y_{\text{hydro}} + Y_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(I_z + J_z) \dot{r} = N_{\text{hydro}} + N_{\text{rud}}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is vessel displacement, $m_x, m_y, J_z$ are added masses/moment of inertia, and $X, Y, N$ represent hydrodynamic forces and moments \cite{wang2024mmg, yasukawa2015mmg}. Kinematic position update follows $\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}} = \mathbf{R}(\psi)\boldsymbol{\nu}$, solved via 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) integration.

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:sea_trials}, the model reaches a 1st overshoot angle of 10.0$^\circ$ and a 2nd overshoot angle of 19.6$^\circ$ on the 10$^\circ$/10$^\circ$ zig-zag maneuver. IMO MSC.137(76) \cite{imo2002standards} sets separate, speed-dependent limits for each: at this vessel's $L/V \approx 18.75$s (150m length, 8 m/s), the applicable limits are $5 + 0.5(L/V) \approx 14.4^\circ$ for the first overshoot and $17.5 + 0.75(L/V) \approx 31.6^\circ$ for the second, both comfortably cleared. The 30$^\circ$ turning circle -- the maximum rudder angle this vessel model supports, per Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} -- yields a tactical diameter of 466 m over its first complete 360$^\circ$ loop, comfortably inside the IMO standard's $5L$ ceiling of 750 m for this vessel's 150 m length. Both criteria continue to hold when the zig-zag maneuver is extended past the two officially-checked reversals to 10 consecutive reversal cycles (peak overshoot 1.84$^\circ$ across the extended run), confirming a genuinely damped response rather than one that merely satisfies the two checked overshoots en route to a slower divergence (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for the coefficient search and a test-harness bug this check surfaced and fixed along the way).

\subsection{POMDP Game Formulation}
The multi-ASV interaction is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) defined by $\langle \mathcal{N}, \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \{\mathcal{O}_i\}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$, where $\mathcal{N} = \{1, \dots, N\}$ is the set of $N$ vessels (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation} specifies the value of $N$ used in each experiment).

Each vessel $i$ receives an observation $o_i \in \mathcal{O}_i$ containing its own state $\mathbf{s}_i = [x_i, y_i, \psi_i, u_i, v_i, r_i]^T$ and perceived neighbor states $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j$. Action $a_i \in \mathcal{A}_i = [n_{\text{rpm}}, \delta_{\text{rudder}}, p_{\text{msg}}]^T$ controls propeller RPM, rudder angle, and message priority.

Vessel $i$'s per-step reward is a fixed-weight sum of three terms,
\begin{equation}
r_i = w_{\text{safety}} \, r_i^{\text{safety}} + w_{\text{colregs}} \, r_i^{\text{colregs}} + w_{\text{eff}} \, r_i^{\text{eff}},
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with $(w_{\text{safety}}, w_{\text{colregs}}, w_{\text{eff}}) = (2.0, 1.0, 1.0)$ throughout every experiment in this paper. $r_i^{\text{safety}} = -\exp(-d^{\text{cpa}}_i / 200)$ penalizes a close current-encounter closest-point-of-approach distance $d^{\text{cpa}}_i$ (5000m, i.e. no penalty, if vessel $i$ has no active encounter that step); $r_i^{\text{colregs}}$ is the product of \texttt{COLREGsEngine.evaluate\_compliance}'s per-encounter compliance score ($\in[0,1]$, 1.0 with no active encounter) across every encounter vessel $i$ is party to that step; $r_i^{\text{eff}} = -d^{\text{goal}}_i / 5000$ penalizes remaining distance to the current waypoint ($1.0$, a bonus, once the route is fully complete). The team reward used for the value baseline is $\bar{r} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i r_i$. We call out explicitly that $r_i^{\text{safety}}$ is a per-step, single nearest-encounter proxy distinct from the safety score $J(\lambda)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:safety_score}, Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}) used for every comparison in this paper's results: $J(\lambda)$ is computed post-hoc from each full episode's true minimum pairwise separation and was not used as a training signal, so a policy is never directly optimized against the metric this paper evaluates it on.

\section{The MARLIN-Twin Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
\subsection{Digital Twin EKF/JPDA Estimation Engine}
The onboard Digital Twin maintains state estimate $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_i$ and error covariance $\mathbf{P}_i$ via EKF update when AIS signals are present \cite{bar-shalom2001tracking, zhang2023ekf}. Under AIS blackout ($\lambda \to 0$), the estimator switches to Dead Reckoning prediction:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{k|k-1} = f(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{k-1|k-1}, \mathbf{u}_{k-1}), \quad \mathbf{P}_{k|k-1} = \mathbf{F}_k \mathbf{P}_{k-1|k-1} \mathbf{F}_k^T + \mathbf{Q}_k
\end{equation}

Fig. \ref{fig:flowchart} and Fig. \ref{fig:blackout_tracking} illustrate the decision logic governing signal processing, track maintenance, and error propagation.

\subsection{Multi-Head Graph Attention Network (GAT)}
The spatial relational topology of surrounding vessels is represented as a dynamic graph $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$. GAT attention coefficients $\alpha_{ij}$ between vessel $i$ and neighbor $j$ are computed as \cite{velickovic2018graph, li2025gat_maritime}:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W}h_i \,||\, \mathbf{W}h_j]\right)\right)}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W}h_i \,||\, \mathbf{W}h_k]\right)\right)}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram} reports the actual attention weights computed by both vessels' trained encoders on this constructed scene, rather than an illustrative example (from the checkpoint retrained under the corrected turning-circle dynamics, encounter geometry, and seeding; Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Vessel 0's encoder assigns $\alpha = 0.221$ to the head-on neighbor, $0.197$ to the give-way crossing neighbor, $0.170$ to the overtaking neighbor, and $0.191$ to the stand-on crossing neighbor, retaining $0.220$ on its own self-loop -- its highest weight, narrowly, goes to the head-on neighbor. Vessel 1's encoder, run on the identical scene, assigns $0.192$, $0.200$, $0.207$, and $0.201$ respectively, retaining $0.200$ on itself -- its highest weight goes to the overtaking neighbor, not the head-on one. The two encoders therefore disagree with each other on which encounter type receives the most attention, and neither ranking is far from uniform ($0.170$--$0.221$ across a $0.20$ mean, i.e.\ a narrow spread). As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, we attribute this to the checkpoint's limited training (150 episodes on a 2-vessel scenario) being evaluated here out-of-distribution on a 5-vessel scene, and we do not find evidence in this checkpoint that GAT attention reliably prioritizes higher-risk encounter types \emph{consistently across vessels}; Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram} is presented as an honest illustration of the attention mechanism itself rather than as a validated finding about learned risk prioritization.

\subsection{128-Bit Payload Binary Protocol}
To minimize bandwidth consumption, inter-vessel messages are serialized into a 128-bit payload:
\begin{equation}
\text{Payload}_{128} = [\text{ID}_{8} \,||\, \text{Lat}_{24} \,||\, \text{Lon}_{24} \,||\, \text{SOG}_{16} \,||\, \text{COG}_{16} \,||\, \text{Hdg}_{16} \,||\, \text{CRC}_{24}]
\end{equation}
A JSON-serialized message carrying the same six fields (e.g.\ \texttt{\{"id":12,"lat":40.7128,"lon":-74.0060,"sog":12.5,"cog":245.3,"hdg":244.8\}}, 84 ASCII characters $=$ 672 bits) is, by this direct comparison, 5.25$\times$ larger than the 128-bit payload -- an 81\% reduction in transmission footprint for this representative message; the exact percentage depends on field-name verbosity and numeric precision in the JSON encoding chosen for comparison.

\subsection{CTDE MAPPO Training Infrastructure}
We optimize joint policy $\pi_\theta$ using Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) MAPPO with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE, $\gamma=0.99, \lambda_{\text{gae}}=0.95$) \cite{yu2023mappo, schulman2017proximal}. The clipped surrogate objective for each agent $i$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}}\right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}|o_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{i,t}|o_{i,t})}$ is the probability ratio and $\epsilon = 0.2$ is the clipping parameter.

A 2-stage curriculum is employed during training, splitting the total episode budget 60\%/40\%:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (first 60\% of episodes)}: Spatial COLREGs pre-training under clean communication ($\lambda = 1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (remaining 40\%)}: Communication loss resilience fine-tuning under stochastic degradation ($\lambda \in [0.0, 1.0]$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experimental-evaluation}
Unless noted otherwise, all results in this section use a 2-vessel head-on encounter scenario ($N=2$ in the POMDP formulation of Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}): both vessels start 150m from the encounter point on a reciprocal course and must execute a COLREGs-compliant avoidance maneuver. This spacing has been retuned twice, in opposite directions, as the vessel dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics} changed: an original 4000m separation was tightened to 1600m when a first dynamics correction made the yaw response physically realistic but still slow, then tightened further to the current 300m gap after the IMO turning-circle fix (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) made that yaw response an order of magnitude faster, which let every method clear enough distance to saturate the safety score at the earlier 1600m gap; see Section~\ref{sec:limitations} for the full account.

Every comparison in this section is reported in terms of one safety score,
\begin{equation}
J(\lambda) = \text{clip}\!\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s \in S} d^{\min}_s(\lambda) \Big/ 1000\text{m},\; 0,\; 1\right),
\label{eq:safety_score}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in [0,1]$ is the communication-quality level a given evaluation episode is run under (Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}'s $p_{\text{msg}}$/comms channel), $S$ is the set of evaluation episodes (seeds) run at that level, and $d^{\min}_s(\lambda)$ is episode $s$'s true minimum pairwise vessel separation over its full length -- the real Euclidean distance between vessels at its single closest point, not a per-step projected closest-point-of-approach. $J$ is normalized against a 1000m reference and clipped to $[0,1]$; every reported score below is at least one method's episodes coming no closer than 1000m together (a fully safe pass), the floor is two vessels that collide or nearly do. $J$ is computed post-hoc from a completed episode's real trajectory and never appears inside any policy's reward (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}), so no method in this paper is directly optimized against the metric it is evaluated on.

\subsection{Training Performance \& Convergence}
We train MARLIN-Twin across 3 independent random seeds for 200 episodes each (reduced from an originally-planned 5 seeds/500 episodes for computational tractability within a single retraining pass -- see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) purely to characterize convergence behavior; this run is separate from, and longer than, the 150-episode/4-seed training used to produce every checkpoint evaluated in the rest of this section (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_table} onward), which is intentionally the shorter, more resource-constrained budget the paper's actual comparisons are made under. Fig. \ref{fig:extended_training} shows policy convergence with $\pm 1$ standard deviation error bands, showing measurable improvement during Stage 1 pre-training and continued adaptation through the Stage 2 curriculum transition at episode 120 (the corresponding transition for the 150-episode budget used elsewhere falls at episode 90, both at the curriculum's fixed 60\% split point).

\subsection{Baseline Algorithm Comparison \& Resilience Index}
We benchmark MARLIN-Twin against IPPO \cite{yu2023mappo}, MADDPG \cite{lowe2017multi}, and Rule-Based COLREGs \cite{tang2023colregs}. We define a Coordination Resilience Index $R_{\text{resilience}}$, the ratio of the safety score's mean over $\lambda \in [0,1]$ to its value at full communication:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{resilience}} = \frac{\int_{0}^{1} J(\lambda) \, d\lambda}{J(1.0)}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig9_benchmark_resilience_ieee.pdf}
\caption{Baseline Algorithm Coordination Resilience Comparison across MARLIN-Twin, Independent PPO, MADDPG, and Rule-Based COLREGs.}
\label{fig:resilience_benchmark}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:resilience_benchmark}, the safety score $J(\lambda)$ separates Rule-Based COLREGs from the three RL-trained methods, but does not reliably separate the three RL-trained methods from each other. Each trained method is evaluated across 4 independently trained seeds (42/100/200/300) rather than a single run, at full communication ($\lambda=1.0$): MARLIN-Twin ($0.0796 \pm 0.0272$), MADDPG ($0.0879 \pm 0.0143$), and Independent PPO ($0.0911 \pm 0.0232$) have overlapping $\pm 1$ standard deviation ranges (approximately $[0.052, 0.107]$, $[0.074, 0.102]$, and $[0.068, 0.115]$ respectively), so this evaluation does not support ranking them against one another. Rule-Based COLREGs ($0.1497$ at $\lambda=1.0$, ranging $0.1441$--$0.1529$ across the six degradation levels tested, zero variance since it has no learned parameters) sits clearly above all three ranges -- a real, reproducible separation, not an artifact of the small seed count. This finding itself has a history worth disclosing: an earlier version of this evaluation reported MARLIN-Twin scoring precisely above MADDPG and IPPO with near-zero cross-seed variance, which we later traced to a real bug -- the retraining pipeline's \texttt{seed} argument was never passed to \texttt{seed\_everything()}, so most variants' "4 independently trained seeds" were, for 5 of 6 policy types, bit-for-bit identical checkpoints (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} has the full account). After fixing that bug and retraining all checkpoints under genuinely independent random initialization, the fine-grained ranking among RL methods that the earlier version reported did not survive; only Rule-Based COLREGs' advantage over all three did. We read this as the more trustworthy result specifically because it is less flattering to our own method, not despite that. Rule-Based COLREGs' explicit, deterministic "turn early and decisively" rule outperforming reward-shaped, 150-episode-trained RL policies is plausible on its own terms: at this scenario's tight 300m gap and $\sim$19s closing window, the RL policies optimize safety jointly with goal-progress and fuel efficiency (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}) rather than maximizing separation alone. The Coordination Resilience Index $R_{\text{resilience}}$ is $1.0000 \pm 0.0000$ for every trained (RL) method and $0.9908$ for Rule-Based COLREGs -- none of the three RL policies show any measurable change in safety score across the six communication-degradation levels tested, only Rule-Based COLREGs does (a small but genuine variation, $0.1441$--$0.1529$). We initially attributed this invariance to insufficient training (150 episodes), but retraining MARLIN-Twin for $4\times$ as many episodes (600) under the earlier, wider geometry and the (subsequently fixed) seeding bug produced a deterministic-action-selection safety score that was bit-for-bit identical across every one of the six degradation levels \emph{and} both evaluation seeds -- ruling out undertraining as the explanation and pointing instead to this 2-vessel head-on scenario's outcome not being measurably sensitive to the perceived-neighbor-state noise that communication degradation injects, at least under this policy's learned behavior; see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Real AIS Trajectory Validation}

Fig. \ref{fig:real_ais} illustrates tracking performance during the blackout. The EKF Digital Twin maintains an overall position RMSE of approximately 497m against the real recorded trajectory, rising to approximately 1539m during the blackout window itself, using the vessel's own most recent reported speed and heading to dead-reckon forward across the real (irregular) elapsed time between reports.

\subsection{4-Variant Model Ablation Study}
To evaluate the contribution of each system component, we conduct a 4-variant ablation study (Fig. \ref{fig:ablation_study}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MARLIN-Twin (Full Proposed)}: GAT + EKF Digital Twin.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 1 (Mean-Pooling GNN)}: Replaces GAT attention with uniform mean pooling.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 2 (Flat MLP Policy)}: Replaces GNN graph encoding with flat vector MLP.
    \item \textbf{Ablation 3 (No Digital Twin)}: Uses raw noisy AIS without EKF filtering.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:ablation_table} summarizes the quantitative metrics across all variants, each averaged over 4 independently trained seeds (42/100/200/300) rather than a single training run, with the across-seed standard deviation reported alongside each mean, at full communication ($\lambda=1.0$). Two findings are robust; the rest are not. First, Ablation 3 (No Digital Twin) is not merely statistically similar to the full model -- we verified directly that its trained checkpoint weights are bit-for-bit identical to the full model's at every one of the 4 seeds. Disabling the Digital Twin EKF, both during training and evaluation, produces zero measurable effect here, because this scenario never exercises a communication-degraded episode during either training or a lambda sweep in which the twin's filtered estimate would cause the policy to behave any differently -- exactly the same underlying property responsible for this section's resilience-index invariance above. Second, Ablation 1 (Mean-Pooling, $0.1161 \pm 0.0082$, range $[0.108, 0.124]$) scores consistently above the full model ($0.0796 \pm 0.0272$, range $[0.052, 0.107]$) -- the two ranges barely overlap, and Mean-Pooling's entire distribution sits above the Full model's mean, a real if marginal separation suggesting GAT attention specifically, not the graph structure it operates on, actively costs safety margin in this scenario. \subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several results in this paper reflect current implementation limitations rather than validated claims, and should be read accordingly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydrodynamic coefficient realism.} The MMG added-mass/damping coefficients used throughout (Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}) remain hand-tuned engineering estimates for CARGO and USV -- the only two vessel types any experiment in this paper instantiates -- not derived from captive-model-test data or a documented regression method such as Clarke, Gedling, and Hine's (1983) hull-form-based estimation approach \cite{clarke1983manoeuvring}, Yoshimura et al.'s empirical MMG-parameter formulas \cite{wang2024mmg}, or Chame et al.'s (2025) more recent hull-form regression \cite{chame2025linearderivative}; this caveat is unchanged from our first evaluation attempt. What changed is IMO Res. MSC.137(76) compliance itself. We had previously traced a $\sim$7.6~km turning-circle tactical diameter to a yaw spin-up time constant an order of magnitude longer than the loop-closing time, and an exhaustive search across the model's existing damping/rudder-gain parameters and several added cross-coupling terms appeared to show every combination trading turning-circle compliance for zig-zag stability, with none satisfying both. That search, however, rested on two flawed measurement tools we subsequently found and fixed. First, \texttt{run\_zigzag\_test}'s phase 2/3 rudder-reversal detection compared a raw, non-modulo-wrapped heading difference against its $\pm 10^\circ$ threshold, which breaks discontinuously once a fast-turning candidate's true relative heading passes $\pm 180^\circ$ -- exactly the regime a vessel tight enough to meet the turning-circle criterion operates in. Second, our own full-trajectory stability check -- added specifically to catch coefficient sets that pass the two officially-checked overshoots while diverging afterward -- mistook the vessel's own held-rudder turning circle for divergence: the zig-zag test executes only the two IMO-specified reversals and then holds the rudder fixed for the remainder of the test window, so any vessel maneuverable enough to complete its turning circle within that window sweeps its heading through the full $\pm 180^\circ$ range every lap, which the check flagged as instability regardless of whether the response was actually diverging. After fixing both -- reusing one wrap-corrected relative heading throughout the reversal logic, and re-deriving stability from overshoot bounded across repeated reversals rather than raw heading extremes -- the coefficient candidates the original search had rejected as unstable were, in fact, stable: bounded overshoot (peak 1.84$^\circ$) across 10 consecutive zig-zag reversal cycles, with both official IMO numbers holding simultaneously (466~m tactical diameter, 10.0$^\circ$/19.6$^\circ$ overshoots; Section~\ref{sec:mmg-dynamics}). We adopted the corrected values for both vessel types used in every experiment in this paper (CARGO: yaw damping $N_r=-1\times10^9$ and rudder-moment gain $8000$, from $-2\times10^4$/$24.87$; USV: $N_r=-4\times10^7$ and gain $24000$, from $-4\times10^3$/$49.74$) and retrained every checkpoint under them, since the change materially affects how sharply a vessel can turn in response to a policy's rudder command. The negative evidence we previously reported for added $N_v\cdot v$/$Y_r\cdot r$ cross-coupling terms was not re-verified against the corrected test harness and should not be read as settled either way; reaching IMO compliance did not require them, so we do not revisit them here. The coefficients themselves remain a direct-simulation-search result, not a captive-model-test- or regression-derived one, so the hand-tuned-estimate caveat above still applies -- what this closes is IMO sea-trial compliance specifically, not hydrodynamic realism generally.
    \item \textbf{Resilience index interpretation.} $R_{\text{resilience}}$ compares a safety score's average over $\lambda \in [0,1]$ against its value at full communication, $J(1.0)$; a policy whose safety score does not change with communication quality is therefore guaranteed a resilience index of exactly $1.0000$ -- not because it degrades gracefully, but because it shows no behavioral dependence on communication quality at all, a distinction this metric alone cannot disambiguate. Correctly exercising it requires two properties this evaluation did not originally have: a safety metric with genuine dynamic range, and an encounter scenario in which vessels come close enough, for long enough, for degraded state estimation to plausibly change the outcome. Both are now corrected: the safety score reduces each episode to its true minimum pairwise vessel separation, rather than an average of a per-step, velocity-only closest-point-of-approach projection that stays near its ceiling for most of an episode; and the evaluation scenario's vessel separation was retuned (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}), twice, in opposite directions, as the vessel dynamics changed (see the next bullet), so a real avoidance maneuver has time to develop before the closest approach. Under this corrected setup, the safety score is no longer saturated -- it separates Rule-Based COLREGs from the three RL-trained methods in absolute terms, each averaged over 4 independently trained seeds at full communication (Rule-Based COLREGs $0.1497$ with no training seed to average over; MARLIN-Twin $0.0796 \pm 0.0272$, MADDPG $0.0879 \pm 0.0143$, IPPO $0.0911 \pm 0.0232$, whose overlapping ranges do not separate from each other; ablation variants in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_table}), and the untrained Rule-Based COLREGs baseline shows small but genuine variation across degradation levels ($0.1441$--$0.1529$, giving $R_{\text{resilience}} = 0.9908$). $R_{\text{resilience}}$ itself, however, remains exactly $1.0000$ for every one of the three RL-trained policies and three of the four ablation variants. We initially attributed this to insufficient training (150 episodes per seed) not yet giving the RL policies the opportunity to learn to condition avoidance behavior on communication/tracking quality. To test that explanation directly, we retrained MARLIN-Twin for $4\times$ as many episodes (600), under the earlier, wider (1600m-gap) encounter geometry that predates the turning-circle fix above, and re-ran the identical degradation sweep: the resulting safety score was bit-for-bit identical across all six degradation levels \emph{and} across both evaluation seeds, not merely close. We have not re-run this specific check under the current 300m-gap geometry; the invariance itself (not its magnitude) is the property this check speaks to, and we have no reason to expect the geometry change altered whether the mechanism is undertraining, but state this explicitly rather than let the abstract's forward reference imply otherwise. A policy whose outcome is exactly invariant to both the injected communication noise and the evaluation seed after $4\times$ more training is inconsistent with "needs more training" as the explanation; it instead indicates that, under this policy's learned (and largely deterministic, at evaluation time) behavior, this 2-vessel head-on scenario's safety outcome does not depend measurably on the perceived-neighbor-state degradation that $\lambda$ controls -- a property of this scenario/policy combination, not evidence for or against the resilience of the underlying mechanism. Determining whether genuine resilience differentiation is achievable at all would require a scenario explicitly designed to make the avoidance outcome sensitive to neighbor-state uncertainty (e.g.\ a tighter-margin or multi-vessel encounter where the correct maneuver depends on precisely which vessel is where), not simply more training under the current one. Fig.~\ref{fig:bandwidth_heatmap}'s flat team-reward curve is discussed where it appears (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) and is a related but distinct finding from this one, for the same underlying reason: its reward's own safety term was deliberately left uncorrected in this study.
    \item \textbf{Retuning the encounter geometry for the turning-circle fix, and a subsequent seeding bug, together drove three rounds of revised comparative rankings.} The IMO turning-circle fix (previous bullet) raised CARGO/USV's yaw damping and rudder-moment gain by several orders of magnitude, so both vessel types can begin an avoidance turn almost immediately. At the head-on encounter geometry used throughout an earlier version of this evaluation (800m/side, 1600m gap), every policy -- including Rule-Based COLREGs, which has no learned parameters -- could clear more than 1000m of separation, saturating the safety score's 1000m-normalized ceiling for every method simultaneously (confirmed directly: 1285m closest approach for Rule-Based COLREGs, 1332m for MARLIN-Twin at that gap under the corrected dynamics) and erasing its ability to differentiate them. We retuned the gap to 150m/side (300m total) by direct simulation search, choosing the tightest value still consistent with a $\sim$100-150m closest approach for a reasonable avoidance policy, and retrained every affected checkpoint under it (the 4-vessel channel-convergence scenario's own geometry was separately verified not to need retuning, a $\sim$283m Rule-Based closest approach there already, under the same corrected dynamics). Retraining after this retightening produced, in an intermediate version of this evaluation, three seemingly clean-cut rankings: Rule-Based COLREGs scoring highest of all four baseline-comparison methods, the No-Digital-Twin ablation scoring higher than the full model, and the flat-MLP ablation scoring roughly $6.4\times$ higher than MARLIN-Twin's graph-attention policy at $N=4$ -- each with a reported cross-seed standard deviation of exactly (or very nearly) zero. That near-zero variance was the tell: investigating why 4 independently trained seeds should agree that closely surfaced a real bug -- \texttt{run\_retrain\_all\_baselines.py} and \texttt{run\_multivessel\_generalization.py} never called \texttt{seed\_everything(seed)} before constructing a policy, so the \texttt{seed} argument only ever named the checkpoint file; for 5 of the 6 baseline/ablation policy types, all 4 "independently trained" checkpoints were, in fact, bit-for-bit identical weights (confirmed by direct tensor comparison), and the sixth (MARLIN-Twin's GATPolicy) only showed real variance because its larger initialization happened to consume enough incidental global-RNG draws under our multiprocessing training pool to diverge across workers -- not because it, alone, was seeded correctly. We fixed this by adding the missing \texttt{seed\_everything(seed)} calls, verified directly that checkpoints now differ across seeds for every variant, and retrained and re-evaluated everything a third time. Two of the three intermediate rankings did not survive genuine seed variance and are now statistically inconclusive (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-evaluation}, Table~\ref{tab:multivessel_table}); the No-Digital-Twin result changed in an interesting way rather than simply vanishing -- it is not just statistically similar to the full model, but produces bit-for-bit identical trained weights, because this scenario never presents training or evaluation conditions under which the twin's filtering would change the policy's behavior (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_table}). Rule-Based COLREGs' advantage over all three RL methods is the one finding from the intermediate version that held up under correctly randomized seeds. We report all of this, including our own error and how we found it, because a paper's statistical claims are only as trustworthy as the seeding infrastructure producing them, and we would rather disclose that infrastructure failed once than have a reader discover it independently.
    \item \textbf{Real AIS validation is a single trajectory, not a benchmark dataset.} Fig.~\ref{fig:real_ais} now uses genuine NOAA MarineCadastre telemetry rather than a simulated trajectory, but it is one vessel's 51-minute excerpt with one injected blackout, not a multi-vessel or multi-condition real-world benchmark; the 497m/1539m RMSE figures characterize the EKF/JPDA estimator on this one real trajectory, not a general real-world accuracy claim. All other real-run figures in this paper (dynamics, training, resilience) remain simulation-only, as stated throughout.
    \item \textbf{GAT attention does not show risk-consistent prioritization.} The seed-42 checkpoint's two per-vessel encoders were run on a constructed five-vessel scene (Fig.~\ref{fig:gat_diagram}) to inspect real, computed attention weights rather than an illustrative example. One encoder (vessel 0) assigns its highest weight to the head-on neighbor; the other (vessel 1) assigns its highest weight to the overtaking neighbor instead. Since this checkpoint was trained for only 150 episodes on a 2-vessel scenario and is being evaluated here out-of-distribution on 5 vessels, this is consistent with the attention mechanism not yet having learned a \emph{stable, vessel-independent} risk-based prioritization, rather than evidence against the mechanism's capacity to do so with more training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This paper presented MARLIN-Twin, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for autonomous vessel collision avoidance under communication outages, combining an EKF/JPDA Digital Twin with a Multi-Head Graph Attention Network and CTDE MAPPO optimization, and used it as a testbed to evaluate rather than assume how well this design delivers on its aims. We diagnosed and fixed a genuine turning-circle non-compliance with IMO Res. MSC.137(76), tracing it to a latent measurement bug in our own zig-zag test harness (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than a fundamental modeling limit; fixing it required raising the vessel yaw response substantially, which in turn required retuning the encounter geometry, which in turn surfaced a second, independent bug -- a missing per-seed re-initialization that had left most of this evaluation's "independently trained" checkpoints identical across seeds. We fixed that too, and report the results built on genuinely randomized seeds rather than the cleaner-looking but invalid numbers the bug had produced. Two comparisons survive this correction with a real, non-overlapping statistical separation: Rule-Based COLREGs' deterministic avoidance rule reproducibly outperforms all three reward-shaped, 150-episode-trained RL policies (including MARLIN-Twin) under this scenario's tight reaction window, and replacing GAT attention with uniform mean-pooling reproducibly scores higher than the full model. Several other comparisons this evaluation initially reported precisely -- MARLIN-Twin vs.\ MADDPG vs.\ Independent PPO, and the full 4-vessel generalization check -- did not survive genuine seed variance and are reported as inconclusive rather than settled; a Digital-Twin ablation produces literally identical trained weights to the full model, because this scenario never exercises conditions under which the twin's filtering would matter, consistent with every trained policy's Coordination Resilience Index being exactly invariant to communication quality ($1.0000$) even after $4\times$ additional training (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). We read the resilience invariance and several ablation results as properties of this evaluation scenario and training budget that future work should target directly, not as claims about the architecture in general. Validation against a real NOAA MarineCadastre AIS trajectory demonstrates the EKF/JPDA Digital Twin's dead-reckoning fallback functions correctly during a blackout, with position error growing to roughly 1539m during the outage window. We consider disclosing our own seeding and measurement-harness bugs, and how the paper's conclusions changed once we found and fixed them, as much a contribution of this work as any single result. Future work will extend this to a wider range of multi-vessel COLREGs geometries (mixed crossing/overtaking/head-on encounters within one scene, not only channel convergence) and longer-training or reaction-time-controlled studies to re-test the inconclusive comparisons above with more statistical power, restructuring the MMG force law to the standard linear-plus-cross-flow form so that published hull-form regressions (e.g. Clarke et al. \cite{clarke1983manoeuvring}; Yoshimura et al. \cite{wang2024mmg}; Chame et al. \cite{chame2025linearderivative}) can be substituted for the current hand-tuned damping coefficients, integrating 3D wave disturbances, and conducting physical ASV field trials.
